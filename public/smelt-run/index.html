03 / Read the water, not just the calendar

What actually starts a run?

A living spring calendar: conditions change, and the uncertainty should change with them.

SIGNAL 01

Ice-out and warming water

Rainbow smelt gather around lake tributaries after ice-out. Spawning can begin as streams approach the low-to-mid 40s°F and continues across a broader spring range. Only direct USGS water measurements receive a stream-temperature label. NOAA buoy and satellite readings describe lake conditions, not spawning streams.

SIGNAL 02

Gentle spring rain

Warming rain is a classic trigger, but high, fast streams can be unsafe. A forecast chance of rain is not evidence that a stream has risen or fish have arrived.

SIGNAL 03

Darkness and fish reports

Spawning moves are often concentrated at night. Watch conditions, then confirm locally before traveling. This tracker never upgrades a region to “run confirmed” without an independent observed-fish source.

The decision rule

Promising conditions mean it could be worth scouting — not that you'll catch smelt. Regions lacking local stream thermometers stay on weather watch, even during a traditional peak week. This matters because Great Lakes smelt abundance is far below its historic highs.

04 / The story behind the run

There is more moving through this creek than smelt.

On a spring night, a dark stretch of water can seem perfectly still. Yet just beneath its surface, a migration may be underway. For the person holding a dip net, it is a chance at a catch. For the fish, it is the brief return to shallow water that makes another generation possible.

A moment on the bank

Before you lower the net, look around.

Listen to the current. Notice the cold coming off the lake, the edge of the receding ice, the different sound of water moving over stones. A few degrees of warming water can help change the behavior of a fish that spent much of the year out of sight.

For generations, smelt dipping has meant late nights, lanterns, wet boots, stories traded on a riverbank, and sometimes a pail of silver fish. Those memories are part of Michigan's outdoor heritage—even when the creek is quiet.

What can a night without smelt tell you about the lake?

Why do they come here?

After ice-out, rainbow smelt move from deeper lake waters toward streams and shorelines to spawn. Temperature, stream conditions and darkness help shape the timing. The forecast shows clues—not a promise that fish will arrive.

A familiar fish, with a complicated history

Rainbow smelt are not native to most of the Great Lakes. Introduced in the early 1900s, they became food for predators such as lake trout and walleye, but they also feed on young fish and can compete with native species. Both sides of that story matter.

Why don't the runs look like they used to?

The enormous catches remembered from earlier decades are not today's baseline. Michigan DNR reports that Great Lakes smelt numbers are much lower than in the past. Their changing abundance reflects a changing food web; a warm night alone cannot bring back a historic run.

Take the experience home. Leave the stream as you found it.

Use lawful public access, respect private property and fragile banks, give other anglers space, and never enter swift or ice-choked water for a better catch. Clean and dry nets, boots and waders before visiting another waterbody; never move live fish or leftover bait between waters. The next person—and the next spring—deserve a healthy shoreline.

A question for your next outing: What did you actually observe—water temperature, flow, ice, weather, fish—and what would you look for next time? The best field notes begin with curiosity, not certainty.

05 / Go prepared

Michigan smelt-dipping rules

Check the actual stream reach, public access, and current DNR digest before dipping; designated trout streams and listed exceptions matter.

March 1 – May 31 · hand nets

Great Lakes, listed connecting waters, and tributaries generally only from the mouth to ½ mile upstream, subject to exceptions. Other inland waters require an explicit listing.

March 20 – May 31 · dip nets

Generally streams except designated trout streams and specifically closed reaches. Dip nets must not exceed 9×9 feet.

Two gallons daily possession

The Michigan DNR smelt possession limit is two gallons. Additional possession and transport restrictions can apply.

Access and safety first

No netting within 100 feet of dams. A map marker does not guarantee legal access. Check designated trout-stream restrictions, private property, fast spring currents, slippery bottoms, and cold water. Carry light, appropriate gear, and a PFD.

06 / Common questions

Smelt dipping FAQ

When is the Michigan smelt run?

Usually in spring, especially April into May along Lake Superior tributaries. Dates vary with water temperatures and ice-out. The tool avoids exact run dates without fish observations.

Which Michigan smelt-dipping spots are historically best?

The DNR's older location inventory lists the Tahquamenon River, Roxbury Creek, Galloway Creek, Anna River, and Falls River among good opportunities. That does not establish the status of fish in any given year.

What water temperature triggers smelt spawning?

Michigan Sea Grant describes initial movement as water reaches around 42–44°F following warming and rain. Smelt may also spawn in warmer water; look for trends, not one magic number.

Does a warm air forecast mean smelt are running?

No. Weather is not stream temperature, and stream temperature is not a fish count. The tracker labels each independently.

Why have the big smelt runs become less common?

Smelt abundance across the Great Lakes is much lower than during its historic peak. Shifts in the food web, predators, and lake conditions all matter; one warm spring does not ensure a large run. Explore the story behind the run.

Can I dip smelt anywhere on a listed river?

No. DNR rules exclude designated trout streams, certain reaches, and dam proximity. Private property requires permission. Check the detailed regulatory map and signs.
